# Mortgage Engagement and Switching: What the Data Shows

## What we set out to understand

We wanted to know whether what a customer does in the weeks after they receive their first email tells us anything about whether they will eventually switch their mortgage. If it does, that has real value: it means the platform can identify likely movers early and act on that signal.

This analysis covers 5,000 mortgages across three lenders -- Elderless, Roast Ham, and California -- using data from May 2024 through June 2026.

---

## The answer is yes, but with an important caveat

There is a clear relationship between customer behaviour and switching. However, the signal is not evenly distributed across the engagement funnel. It concentrates in one specific action: when a customer explicitly declares they are considering switching, they follow through at a dramatically higher rate than customers who never reach that point.

Everything that happens before -- clicking the email, logging in, reading content -- shows a positive pattern, but these earlier steps do not reliably separate switchers from non-switchers on their own. They tend to accompany intent, not drive it. A customer who is going to declare intent will usually have clicked and logged in too. But not all customers who click and log in will go on to declare intent.

The practical implication is that intent is not just a milestone in the funnel. It is the signal that matters most.

---

## When a customer engages matters as much as whether they engage

Looking at the timing of engagement across the first three months reveals something worth paying attention to. Customers who interacted with the platform in month 1 -- the first four weeks after their initial email -- showed higher rates of intent and switching than customers who only engaged in months 2 or 3.

Late engagement tends to be reactive. By the time a customer reaches out in month 3, they may already be mid-decision. Month 1 engagement looks more like genuine early discovery -- customers who are curious and open. That distinction matters for how outreach is designed.

If month 1 engagement is consistently the better predictor, the quality of the first contact is more important than the volume of follow-up.

---

## Most customers drop off right after the first email

Of the 860 mortgages that received at least one email, the large majority never clicked, logged in, or took any further action. The biggest gap in the funnel is at the very first step: moving from "received an email" to "did anything with it."

Improving conversion at that point would have more impact than optimising anything further down the funnel, simply because it is where the most customers are lost.

---

## One more thing: most of the mortgage book is not being contacted at all

Only 860 of the 5,000 mortgages in this dataset -- 17% -- received any email contact. The other 83% left no trace in the engagement data. All of the findings above apply to that 17%. We have no signal on the rest.

This matters for two reasons. First, it limits how much we can generalise. Second, and more importantly, it raises a question: why are so many mortgages outside the programme? If the answer is a lender configuration issue or a data gap, that is worth fixing before drawing strong conclusions about what the data does or does not show.

---

## Lender outcomes are quite different

Elderless customers switch at a 43% rate; Roast Ham customers switch at 31%. Roast Ham customers redeem at a much higher rate (42%) than Elderless (24%). California has only four mortgages in the dataset, which is too small to read anything into.

A 12-point gap in switch rate between lenders is significant. It likely reflects a mix of product design, customer segment, and how each lender is using the platform. Understanding which of those factors is driving the difference would be a useful next step.

---

## What to do with this

Based on what the data shows, three things stand out as worth acting on:

**1. Treat intent as a conversion event, not just a data point.**
When a customer declares intent, they are close to a decision. Having a clear, fast follow-up at that moment -- whether from an adviser, an automated offer, or a well-timed call -- is likely to have an outsized effect relative to anything earlier in the funnel.

**2. Focus on the quality of the first contact.**
Month 1 engagement predicts outcomes better than later engagement. The subject line, timing, and relevance of the first email are the highest-leverage variables to test. Volume of follow-up is a secondary concern.

**3. Understand why 83% of the mortgage book is unreached.**
Before optimising the engagement programme, it is worth asking whether the reach problem is solvable. If there is a configuration or data issue limiting which mortgages get emailed, fixing it would expand the programme's potential impact considerably.

---

## What would sharpen these recommendations

We are confident in the direction of these findings -- the intent signal is clear and consistent across cohorts. But there are a few things missing from the data that would make the recommendations more precise:

**The rate each customer is on, and what competing rates look like.** A customer whose current rate is already close to the best available market rate has little financial reason to switch, regardless of how engaged they are. Without this, we cannot fully separate platform engagement effects from rate environment effects. This is probably the single most important data gap.

**Whether customers are near the end of their fixed term.** Switching only makes sense at certain points in a mortgage lifecycle. Knowing where each customer sits relative to their expiry would help focus outreach on the right people at the right time.

**Other contact channels.** The data only includes email. If customers are also receiving SMS, push notifications, or adviser contact, those interactions are invisible here. A customer who switched after a phone call looks like an organic mover in this dataset.

**Why customers did not switch.** The intent event tells us a customer was considering it. The data does not tell us why they stopped. Customer feedback, even informal, would help distinguish customers who were close and needed a nudge from those who decided against it for structural reasons.

These are the gaps that would turn a directional finding into a confident recommendation.
